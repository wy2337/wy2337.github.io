Understanding how context shapes judgments

A four-state vignette survey examining how people with different residential contexts evaluate the same specified decisions.

The research question

Broad opinion questions can leave respondents imagining different situations. This study used concrete sentencing scenarios to ask whether adults who describe their communities as rural, suburban, or urban evaluate the same decisions differently.

The case demonstrates a measurement problem relevant to research on complex decisions: a shared rating scale is more informative when participants also share a clear understanding of what they are evaluating.

The central finding

Residential context was associated with a modest difference in ratings. The size and uncertainty of that difference mattered more than a simple ranking of groups.

How the study worked

The online survey used Qualtrics and Prolific to recruit English-speaking adults in Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, and Pennsylvania. The complete-case sample comprised 975 respondents: 94 rural, 511 suburban, and 370 urban.

Participants read seven legally informed fictional crime scenarios, each with a specified sentence. They rated that sentence on a seven-point scale from too lenient to too harsh.

Analysis choices

  • Account for repeated responses. Standard errors were clustered by respondent.
  • Separate context from other differences. Models included state and vignette fixed effects plus measured demographic, contextual, and attitudinal controls.
  • Check the measurement assumption. An ordered-logit model examined the ordinal rating outcome alongside the primary OLS analysis.
  • Check scenario variation. Interaction tests examined whether residence-related patterns differed across vignette types.

What the evidence showed

After adjustment, suburban respondents rated the specified sentences 0.194 points closer to “too harsh” than rural respondents. This is a modest difference on a seven-point scale.

The urban–rural comparison was less conclusive in the primary model. Both estimates remained positive in ordered logit, but their significance patterns were not identical across models.

The residence-by-vignette interaction test was not significant (p = .323). That does not prove the groups respond identically across all scenarios.

Relevance to product research

This is a social-science survey study, not a product usability evaluation. Its contribution to a UX portfolio is the approach to measurement, segmentation, and uncertainty.

  • Use a common scenario. Give participants enough context to make ratings interpretable and comparable.
  • Define the construct precisely. An evaluation of one decision differs from a general attitude toward an institution or system.
  • Check subgroup results carefully. Report effect size and uncertainty, especially when subgroup sizes are unequal.
  • Follow quantitative differences with explanation. Qualitative research could investigate why people interpret the same information differently.

What I would test next

For a complex decision-support product, pair a standardized scenario with task observation and follow-up interviews. Examine comprehension and decision quality as well as ratings. This is a proposed application of the method, not an outcome measured in the sentencing study.

Limits and interpretation

The four-state Prolific sample is not nationally representative, and the rural subgroup includes only 94 respondents. Residential category is self-reported. Cross-sectional associations cannot establish that residence causes a judgment.

Fictional scenarios simplify real decisions. The study does not measure product usability, task success, or behavior after a product launch.
