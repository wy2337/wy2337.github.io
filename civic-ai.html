Evaluating conversational AI beyond user ratings

A field comparison of generative and rule-based chatbots for collecting meaningful feedback from park visitors.

Sample

The research question

Collecting feedback in a public space calls for an experience people can use quickly, on their own phones, while sharing useful details. Could a generative chatbot support that task better than a rule-based alternative?

The study compared ParkGen, a generative AI chatbot, with ParkChat, a rule-based chatbot. It examined attitudes toward the tools and the conversations they produced.

The central finding

Higher trust and empathy ratings did not come with a detectable increase in response length. Perceptions and behavior needed to be evaluated together.

How the study worked

Visitors were recruited on site in July and August 2024. Each participant used both chatbots in randomized order, on their own smartphone, and completed a survey after each experience. The interface and 12-question framework were held consistent.

50participants in the final field-study sample
1,059user messages across 100 conversations

The study combined seven-point survey ratings, message logs, manual response-quality ratings, keyword and text analysis, and mixed-effects models with participant random intercepts. Paired comparisons used the 50-person sample; the complete-case modeling sample contained 33 participants and 792 responses.

Two researchers independently assessed response quality. Response length correlated with these ratings (Spearman ρ = .73), making it a useful proxy to investigate—without treating word count as equivalent to useful feedback.

What the evidence showed

1. The generative chatbot felt more empathetic and trustworthy.

ParkGen received higher ratings for empathy (p = .005), trust (p = .008), acceptance (p = .004), and anthropomorphism (p = .016). Differences in willingness to use and positive feelings were not significant at the .05 level.

2. Better ratings did not translate into longer responses.

There was no significant difference in message length (p = .43). The generative condition collected fewer messages overall and showed a steeper decline in completion across the conversation.

3. Conversational warmth could introduce friction.

Participant feedback described excessive wording, artificial human presentation, and misinterpretation. A documented example also showed a generated question drifting from suggestions for improvement to what a visitor liked. That example identifies a design risk; it does not establish how often the issue occurred.

Implications for product decisions

These are research-based recommendations, not documented changes to a launched product.

  • Use a balanced evaluation. Measure attitudes, completion, and useful information captured together.
  • Test shorter conversations. Compare concise acknowledgments and prompts against more expansive generative responses.
  • Check question fidelity. A fluent follow-up should still collect the information the research question requires.
  • Investigate fatigue. Vary question order and reduce repetition to separate topic effects from abandonment later in the experience.

What I would test next

Compare a shorter conversational variant with the current approach. Define success criteria in advance for completion, human-rated information quality, and user-reported trust, then evaluate the experience with a broader population.

Limits and interpretation

This was a pilot study in one park with a relatively young sample. English and smartphone requirements constrained participation. Experiencing both chatbots supported within-person comparison but also introduced possible repetition and fatigue.

The smaller complete-case modeling sample further limits generalization. These results do not establish higher engagement or feedback quality from generative AI, and the manuscript does not document downstream operational impact.
